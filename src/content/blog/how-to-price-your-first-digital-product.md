Pricing your first digital product feels like guessing. Price it too low and you work hard for very little. Price it too high and nobody buys. The good news is that your first price doesn't have to be perfect. It has to be **a starting point you can learn from**.

This guide walks you through a simple way to set that first price, test it, and know when it's time to raise it.

## Start with the result, not the hours

Most new creators price by effort: "This took me two weeks, so it should cost a lot." Buyers don't see your hours. They see what the product does for them.

Ask yourself one question: *what changes for the person who buys this?* A pack of lesson plans saves a teacher a weekend of planning. A workout guide gives someone a plan they can follow from day one. That change is what people pay for.

![A smiling creator holding up a camera](/assets/img/creator-camera.webp "Price the change your product makes, not the hours it took to make.")

## Three numbers to check before you set a price

Before you pick a figure, look at three things:

1. **What similar products cost.** Find three or four products like yours and note their prices. You don't have to match them, but you should know the range.
2. **What your audience can afford.** A student audience and a business audience have very different budgets.
3. **What you need to earn.** Decide how much you want to make in your first month, then work out how many sales that takes at each price.

### A quick example

Say you want to earn ₦100,000 in your first month. At ₦5,000 you need 20 sales. At ₦10,000 you need 10. Ask yourself which of those feels realistic for the audience you have today.

> Your first price is not a promise. It's an experiment that tells you what your audience values.

## Try a launch price

A launch price gives early buyers a reason to act now, and gives you real sales to learn from. Keep it simple:

- Pick a regular price, then offer your first buyers a lower one for a set time, such as one week.
- Tell people when the launch price ends, and keep that promise.
- Ask your first buyers what made them buy. Their answers are your best marketing copy.

## When to raise your price

Raise your price when the signs tell you to, not when you feel brave. Good signs include:

- People buy without asking questions first.
- Buyers tell you it was worth more than they paid.
- You've added new material since launch.

When you raise it, tell your audience ahead of time. A clear "the price goes up on Friday" often brings in the people who were waiting.
